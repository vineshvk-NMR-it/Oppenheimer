1The Big Idea: Separate the Fast from the Slow

A molecule is a swarm of light, fast electrons and a few heavy, sluggish nuclei. A proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron, so from the electrons' point of view the nuclei barely move at all. Born and Oppenheimer turned this mass gap into a recipe: freeze the nuclei, solve for the electrons, then let the nuclei move on the energy landscape the electrons create.

The full molecular Hamiltonian couples everything together:

Ĥ = T̂n + T̂e + V̂ee + V̂en + V̂nn
T̂nnuclear kinetic energy (the slow, heavy part)
T̂eelectronic kinetic energy (the fast, light part)
V̂ee, V̂en, V̂nnelectron–electron, electron–nucleus, nucleus–nucleus Coulomb terms

The approximation writes the total wavefunction as a product, electrons times nuclei:

Ψ(r, R) ≈ φelec(r; R) × χnuc(R)

The semicolon in φ(r; R) is the whole point: the electronic wavefunction depends on nuclear position R only as a parameter, not a variable. This splits one hard problem into two easier ones, which you will solve yourself below.

Step 1 — Electronic. Clamp the nuclei at separation R (so T̂n = 0). Solve
[ T̂e + V̂ee + V̂en + V̂nn ] φ = U(R) φ
Repeat for many R to trace out the potential energy curve U(R).
Step 2 — Nuclear. Feed that curve in as the potential and solve for nuclear motion:
[ T̂n + U(R) ] χ = E χ
The bound solutions are the vibrational energy levels and wavefunctions.

The worked example throughout is the simplest real molecule, the hydrogen molecular ion H2+ (two protons, one electron). Everything below is computed live, not pre-drawn.

2Step 1 · Solve the Electrons at Fixed R

Hold the two nuclei a distance R apart. We solve the electronic Schrödinger equation with the LCAO method: build the molecular orbital from two atomic 1s orbitals, φ± ∝ 1sA ± 1sB, and compute its energy. Drag R and watch the electron cloud reshape; each setting gives one point on the potential curve.

Notice the physics the cloud is showing: in the bonding orbital, electron density piles up between the nuclei, gluing them together, so U(R) develops a minimum. In the antibonding orbital there is a node between the nuclei, density is pushed to the outside, and U*(R) just falls as R grows: no bond. The attractive well of the bonding curve is exactly the potential the nuclei will live in next.

3Step 2 · Solve the Nuclei on U(R)

Now take the bonding curve U(R) built above and treat it as the potential for the two nuclei. Solving the nuclear Schrödinger equation on this well gives a ladder of quantised vibrational levels Ev and their wavefunctions χv(R). This is done here by numerically integrating the equation on the computed curve, not by assuming a formula.

Two features to point out to students. First, the lowest level sits above the bottom of the well: the zero-point energy, a purely quantum effect, so the molecule is never truly at rest. Second, the rungs get closer together as you climb: the well is anharmonic (softer than a parabola near dissociation), so a harmonic-oscillator model is only good near the bottom. Switch to the heavier nuclei and watch every level drop and bunch up, because spacing scales as 1/√μ.

Model note: the LCAO curve is the textbook first approximation, so its well (De ≈ 1.76 eV, ωe ≈ 1700 cm⁻¹) is a little shallow and soft compared with the exact H2+ values (2.79 eV, 2321 cm⁻¹). The method shown is exactly the one used in full electronic-structure calculations; only the quality of the electronic wavefunction differs.

4Why It Works, and When It Breaks

The approximation rests on the nuclei being slow enough that the electrons always relax to their ground state for the current geometry (the adiabatic picture). The small parameter is roughly (me/Mn)1/4, which is about 0.15 even for hydrogen, so the separation is remarkably good for ground-state structure, spectra and most of chemistry.

It breaks down when two electronic potential curves come close together or cross, for example at avoided crossings and conical intersections. There the nuclear motion can kick the system from one electronic state to another (non-adiabatic coupling), and the clean product form Ψ = φχ no longer holds. These situations govern photochemistry, radiationless decay, and charge transfer, which is why going beyond Born–Oppenheimer is an active field.
